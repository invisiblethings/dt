<p>If you already play guitar, you have a big advantage when it comes to learning piano.</p>
<p>Think of an old Elvis Presley film. Elvis is singing a slow ballad and somewhere in the background a guitarist is simply strumming a few chords.</p>
<p>Nothing complicated. The guitar is just providing the harmony and rhythm underneath the voice.</p>

<p>You can do exactly the same thing on piano.</p>
<p>You don&#8217;t always need to play the melody or have both hands moving all over the keyboard. You can simply play chords and use them to accompany a singer — or yourself.</p>
<p>In fact, this is very similar to the way Pianoforall begins.</p>
<h2>Why Guitarists Learn Piano So Quickly</h2>
<p>If you play guitar, many of the things you&#8217;ll learn on piano will already be familiar.</p>
<p><strong>You understand chords.</strong> C major is still C major. The notes and the way you play them are different, but the chord itself is the same.</p>
<p><strong>You understand rhythm.</strong> If you&#8217;re used to strumming different rhythm patterns on guitar, you can apply exactly the same idea to piano.</p>
<p><strong>You&#8217;ll recognise chord progressions.</strong> You&#8217;ve probably played the same familiar chord changes in dozens of different songs.</p>
<p>So you&#8217;re not really starting again. You&#8217;re learning how to use musical ideas you already understand on a different instrument.</p>
<h2>Playing Piano Like a Guitarist</h2>
<p>One of the first things I teach in Pianoforall is what I call Rhythm Style Piano.</p>
<p>The idea is very similar to strumming a guitar.</p>
<p>You learn a few simple chords and then learn different rhythm patterns that you can use with them.</p>
<p>Start with C, F, G, Am and Dm, for example.</p>
<p>Play the chords simply at first.</p>
<p>Then try playing them with a rhythm.</p>
<p>Then gradually add different patterns, bass notes, broken chords and little fills.</p>
<p>Each new exercise adds something to what you can already do.</p>
<p>And because these are skills rather than individual songs, you can use them with hundreds of different songs.</p>
<h2>Piano Makes Chords Easy to See</h2>
<p>Another thing I like about piano is that everything is laid out very clearly in front of you.</p>
<p>On guitar, the same note can appear in several different places on the fretboard.</p>
<p>On piano, the notes simply run from left to right and repeat in the same pattern all the way up the keyboard.</p>
<p>That makes chords, scales and the relationships between notes very easy to see.</p>
<p>If you&#8217;re interested in songwriting or arranging, that can be particularly useful.</p>
<h2>Do You Have to Learn to Read Music?</h2>
<p>Not to begin with.</p>
<p>If you&#8217;ve learned guitar mainly from chords, listening and playing along with songs, you&#8217;ll probably feel quite at home with the way Pianoforall starts.</p>
<p>We begin with chords and rhythms and learn mainly by listening, watching and playing.</p>
<p>Reading music is introduced gradually as you progress.</p>
<p>So you can start making music on the piano without having to go back to square one and spend months learning notation before you&#8217;re allowed to play anything.</p>
<h2>You Don&#8217;t Need to Start From Scratch</h2>
<p>If you already play guitar, I&#8217;d make use of what you know.</p>
<p>Take a chord progression you can already play on guitar and find those same chords on the piano.</p>
<p>Then try playing them with a simple rhythm.</p>
<p>You&#8217;ll probably be surprised by how quickly it starts to make sense.</p>
<p>And once you&#8217;re comfortable with that, you can gradually add new rhythm patterns, ballad styles, blues, improvisation and eventually reading music.</p>
