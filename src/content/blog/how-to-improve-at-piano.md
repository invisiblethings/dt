<p>If you want to improve your piano playing, one of the most useful things you can learn is how to practise properly.</p>
<p>And one of the simplest and most effective ways to practise is focused <strong>repetition</strong>.</p>
<p>If you&#8217;re struggling with a chord change, a rhythm or a particular bar of music, don&#8217;t keep playing the whole piece from beginning to end and hoping the difficult bit will eventually sort itself out.</p>
<p>Stop at the difficult bit.</p>
<p>Slow it down.</p>
<p>Work out exactly what&#8217;s causing the problem and repeat that small section until it starts to feel comfortable.</p>
<h2>Divide and Conquer</h2>
<p>I use this idea throughout Pianoforall and Classics By Ear. I call it <strong>Divide and Conquer.</strong></p>
<p>Instead of thinking, I have to learn this whole piece, divide it into very small sections.</p>
<p>It might be two bars.</p>
<p>It might be one bar.</p>
<p>Sometimes it might only be two chords or a few notes.</p>
<p>Practise that tiny section slowly and repeatedly. Once it feels comfortable, add the next small section and join the two together.</p>
<p>A piece that initially looks complicated becomes much less daunting when you approach it this way.</p>
<h2>Why Repetition Works</h2>
<p>When you first try a new chord, rhythm or fingering pattern, your hands don&#8217;t know what to do. You have to think about almost every movement.</p>
<p>But each time you repeat the movement, it becomes a little more familiar.</p>
<p>Eventually you don&#8217;t have to consciously think about every finger. Your hands begin to recognise the shape and movement automatically.</p>
<p>This is often called <strong>muscle memory</strong>, although the learning is really happening in the brain and nervous system rather than in the muscles themselves.</p>
<p>It&#8217;s the same reason you don&#8217;t have to think about every movement when you tie your shoelaces, type your name or use a knife and fork. You&#8217;ve repeated those movements so many times that they have become automatic.</p>
<p>Piano playing develops in much the same way.</p>
<h2>Practise the Part That Needs Practising</h2>
<p>A very common mistake is to play a piece from beginning to end every time you practise it.</p>
<p>Suppose you can already play 90% of a piece quite comfortably, but there are two bars where you always make a mistake.</p>
<p>If you play the entire piece ten times, you&#8217;ve spent most of your practice time repeating something you can already play.</p>
<p>It makes much more sense to spend those few minutes on the two troublesome bars.</p>
<p>Play them slowly. Work out what is going wrong. Repeat them several times. Take a short break and then try them again.</p>
<p>Once they&#8217;re comfortable, put them back into the piece.</p>
<p>This is a much more efficient way to practise.</p>
<h2>Slow Down Before You Speed Up</h2>
<p>Another common mistake is trying to play something at the finished speed before your fingers are ready.</p>
<p>There&#8217;s no prize for playing a difficult section quickly and getting it wrong twenty times.</p>
<p>Slow it down until you can play it comfortably.</p>
<p>At a slower speed you have time to notice what your fingers are doing, where the chord changes happen and which movements are causing difficulty.</p>
<p>Once the movement feels easy, gradually increase the speed.</p>
<p>Strangely enough, practising slowly is often the quickest way to learn to play quickly.</p>
<h2>Piano Practice Is a Bit Like Learning to Juggle</h2>
<p>Imagine you&#8217;re learning to juggle.</p>
<p>You throw the balls in the air, drop one after five seconds and then go away and do something else.</p>
<p>Ten minutes later you come back, try again for another few seconds, drop the ball and stop.</p>
<p>You&#8217;d probably never get very good at juggling.</p>
<p>Instead, you&#8217;d repeat the same movement again and again while your brain works out the timing.</p>
<p>Piano is much the same.</p>
<p>If a chord change is difficult, spend a few minutes just moving between those two chords.</p>
<p>If a rhythm keeps going wrong, forget the rest of the piece for a moment and concentrate on that rhythm.</p>
<p>If one bar of a classical piece is causing trouble, that bar becomes your entire piece for the next few minutes.</p>
<h2>Don&#8217;t Practise Mistakes</h2>
<p>Repetition is powerful, but there&#8217;s an important catch.</p>
<p><strong>You want to repeat the correct movement, not the mistake.</strong></p>
<p>If you play something incorrectly ten times at full speed, you&#8217;re also practising. Unfortunately, you&#8217;re practising how to play it incorrectly.</p>
<p>That&#8217;s why slowing down matters.</p>
<p>Find a speed where you can play the section correctly and comfortably. Repeat it there until the movement starts to become familiar.</p>
<p>Then gradually bring it up to speed.</p>
<h2>Come Back Tomorrow</h2>
<p>Something else happens when you practise that can be quite surprising.</p>
<p>You may work on a difficult section for ten minutes, get it reasonably comfortable and then stop.</p>
<p>When you come back the following day, it can sometimes feel easier than it did when you left it.</p>
<p>Your brain continues consolidating what you&#8217;ve practised after you&#8217;ve finished playing, particularly during rest and sleep.</p>
<p>So don&#8217;t feel that you have to conquer everything in one sitting.</p>
<p>A few focused minutes today, followed by another few minutes tomorrow, can be much more effective than struggling with something for an hour.</p>
<h2>Keep Your Hands Relaxed</h2>
<p>Repetition doesn&#8217;t mean hammering away at something until your hands hurt.</p>
<p>If you feel yourself becoming tense, slow down.</p>
<p>Let your shoulders drop. Loosen your wrists and hands. Take a breath and try the section again.</p>
<p>You are trying to teach your hands an easy, comfortable movement. Repeating it while you&#8217;re tense can simply teach your body to associate that passage with tension.</p>
<p>Short, relaxed repetitions are much more useful.</p>
<h2>A Simple Way to Practise</h2>
<p>When you come across something difficult:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Isolate it.</strong> Find the smallest part that&#8217;s causing the problem.</li>
<li><strong>Slow it down.</strong> Play at a speed where you can get it right.</li>
<li><strong>Repeat it.</strong> Give your hands time to become familiar with the movement.</li>
<li><strong>Stay relaxed.</strong> Don&#8217;t force your fingers or rush.</li>
<li><strong>Add a little more.</strong> Once it&#8217;s comfortable, join it to the bar before or after it.</li>
<li><strong>Come back tomorrow.</strong> Give your brain time to absorb what you&#8217;ve learned.</li>
</ol>
<p>You can use this approach for almost anything — chord changes, rhythm patterns, scales, improvisation, reading music or learning a difficult classical piece.</p>
<h2>To Sum Up</h2>
<p>Getting better at piano isn&#8217;t simply about spending more time practising. It&#8217;s about using your practice time well.</p>
<p>When something is difficult, <strong>divide and conquer.</strong> Break it into the smallest useful section, slow it down and repeat it until it becomes familiar.</p>
<p>Then add the next little piece.</p>
<p>It&#8217;s a very simple idea, but it&#8217;s one of the most effective ways I know to learn piano.</p>
