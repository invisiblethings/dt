<p>If you want to be able to sit down at a piano and play for family or friends, it&#8217;s worth building up a repertoire of songs you know really well.</p>
<p>They don&#8217;t need to be difficult songs. In fact, some of the simplest songs are often the best ones to have ready, particularly if other people can sing along.</p>
<p>Here&#8217;s how I would go about putting a repertoire together.</p>
<h2>Start With a List of Songs</h2>
<p>Make a list of songs you&#8217;d enjoy playing.</p>
<p>Don&#8217;t worry too much at this stage about whether they&#8217;re easy or difficult. Just get a reasonable list together and then start looking for the chords and lyrics.</p>
<p>Sites such as Ultimate Guitar can be useful for this, although always check the chords yourself. Online chord sheets aren&#8217;t necessarily correct.</p>
<h2>Make Your Own Song Sheets</h2>
<p>I like having my own simple chord and lyric sheets.</p>
<p>You can make these in Word, with the chords written above the appropriate words. A fixed-width font such as Courier makes it much easier to keep everything lined up.</p>
<p>I also like putting the chords in red so they&#8217;re easy to see at a glance.</p>
<p>Print out working copies and play through each song, correcting anything that doesn&#8217;t sound right.</p>
<p>If you&#8217;re unsure about a chord, compare a few different versions online or check the original recording.</p>
<h2>Find a Rhythm That Suits the Song</h2>
<p>If your keyboard has built-in rhythms, try a few and find one that suits each song.</p>
<p>Make a note of the rhythm or style you&#8217;ve chosen on the song sheet. That way, when you come back to the song later, you don&#8217;t have to spend ten minutes trying to remember which one you used.</p>
<p>If you&#8217;re singing as well, you may also need to change the key to suit your voice.</p>
<p>Most keyboards have a transpose function, so if you&#8217;ve moved a song up or down, write the transpose setting on the sheet too.</p>
<p>It&#8217;s amazing how quickly you forget these things.</p>
<h2>Get Everything Ready Before You Need It</h2>
<p>If you&#8217;re likely to play with friends or family, keep your songs together in a folder and put them in a sensible order.</p>
<p>You could also print a couple of spare copies of the lyrics and chords. Then other people can sing along or someone with a guitar can join in.</p>
<p>A little preparation makes the whole thing much more relaxed.</p>
<h2>Keep the Songs You Can Play Well</h2>
<p>Your repertoire doesn&#8217;t need to contain every song you&#8217;ve ever learned.</p>
<p>I&#8217;d rather have twenty songs I can play comfortably than a hundred that I can only half remember.</p>
<p>If there&#8217;s a song where the verse is fine but you always come unstuck in the chorus, take it out of your main folder for now and keep working on it.</p>
<p>Put it back when you can play it comfortably.</p>
<h2>Think About Who You&#8217;re Playing For</h2>
<p>Different songs work for different occasions.</p>
<p>If people want to sing, familiar songs with fairly simple chord progressions and memorable choruses usually work best.</p>
<p>The Beatles, ABBA, Elton John and The Carpenters give you plenty to choose from, and it&#8217;s worth having some newer songs as well.</p>
<p>And don&#8217;t forget the really old songs. At the right gathering, something like <em>Knees Up Mother Brown</em> can be considerably more useful than your finest piece of Chopin.</p>
<p><em>Hey Jude</em> is another useful one because once you reach the end, you can keep everyone going for as long as they&#8217;ll tolerate you.</p>
<p>Blues is useful too. A simple blues progression is easy to play, easy for another musician to join and gives you plenty of room to improvise.</p>
<h2>Have an Easy First Song</h2>
<p>If you&#8217;re nervous about playing in front of people, don&#8217;t start with the most difficult thing you know.</p>
<p>Start with something you can play almost without thinking.</p>
<p>Once you&#8217;ve played one song successfully, you&#8217;ll usually relax and the rest becomes much easier.</p>
<h2>Include Something for Different Ages</h2>
<p><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/group-seniors-standing-by-piano-singing-together.jpg" alt="A group of older adults singing together around a piano" width="1286" height="858" /></p>
<p>If you&#8217;re building a repertoire for parties or family gatherings, it&#8217;s worth having music from different periods.</p>
<p>You might have some Beatles and Elton John, some ABBA, a few more recent songs and some old singalong favourites.</p>
<p>You don&#8217;t need dozens from every period. Just enough that you&#8217;re not completely stumped when someone twenty years younger or forty years older than you asks for something.</p>
<h2>Keep Adding to It</h2>
<p>Your repertoire will change over time.</p>
<p>Some songs will disappear because you never play them. Others will become favourites. Every now and then you&#8217;ll hear something and think, <em>that would be a good one to add to the folder.</em></p>
<p>That&#8217;s really all a repertoire is — a collection of music you enjoy playing and know well enough that you don&#8217;t have to worry about it.</p>
<p>And that&#8217;s also one of the reasons I put so much emphasis in Pianoforall on learning <strong>chords and rhythm patterns that can be applied to lots of different songs.</strong></p>
<p>The more of those patterns you know, the easier it becomes to hear a song, find the chords and turn it into something you can play.</p>
<p>A good repertoire isn&#8217;t really about showing people how clever you are at the piano.</p>
<p>It&#8217;s about being able to sit down and make some music when the opportunity arises.</p>
