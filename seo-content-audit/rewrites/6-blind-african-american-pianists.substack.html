Last reviewed: October 2026

This list profiles seven blind Black musicians who shaped American music: Thomas "Blind Tom" Wiggins, Ray Charles, Art Tatum, Blind Willie Johnson, Blind John Davis, Stevie Wonder and Blind Willie McTell. Five of them made their names as piano players: Wiggins, Tatum, Davis, Charles and Wonder. Blind Willie Johnson sang gospel and played slide guitar, and Blind Willie McTell played twelve-string guitar and sang the blues.

Ray Charles and Stevie Wonder won fame as singers as much as keyboard players, and Johnson and McTell built their records on voice and guitar. The order carries no ranking.

Overcoming adversity: how blind Black musicians excelled

These musicians lived through slavery, segregation or both. After the Civil War, state and local governments passed Jim Crow laws that legalised segregation and denied Black Americans the vote, jobs and education. Those laws stood for about a hundred years, until the 1960s.

Blindness added a second barrier. Some of these musicians trained at schools for blind students: Ray Charles in St. Augustine, Florida, and McTell at schools in Georgia, New York and Michigan. Others learned from relatives, neighbours and church.

### 1. Thomas "Blind Tom" Wiggins (1849-1908) - An Enslaved Pianist and Composer

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T6vLl-55szE

Thomas Wiggins entered the world blind and enslaved on 25 May 1849, near Columbus, Georgia. Some biographies place his birth in Harris County. Months later, his enslaver sold him, his parents Charity and Domingo Wiggins and two older siblings at auction to General James Bethune, a Columbus lawyer.

Tom listened to the Bethune children practise and began to reproduce their music at the piano. Bethune arranged lessons, and at about nine or ten hired him out to a travelling showman, Perry Oliver, and some tours ran to four performances a day. In 1861 Tom composed "The Battle of Manassas", a piano piece about the Confederate victory that year. Much of the concert money from this period went to aid Confederate soldiers.

After emancipation, James Bethune got Tom's parents to sign a guardianship agreement, and the Bethunes kept control of him until his death. His mother tried and failed to win him back. Bethune's son John managed his tours and, in the New Georgia Encyclopedia's words, used Tom's "considerable income" to fund an "extravagant lifestyle". After John died in 1884, a court fight handed control in 1887 to John's estranged wife, Eliza Bethune. The Library of Congress notes that his concerts and sheet music earned his guardians the equivalent of millions of dollars today. Some writers called him "the last slave".

The New Georgia Encyclopedia and Deirdre O'Connell's biography The Ballad of Blind Tom, Slave Pianist suggest he was autistic, a condition undiagnosed in his time. He spent his last years in Eliza Bethune's house in Hoboken, New Jersey, which his earnings had paid for, and died after a stroke on 13 June 1908.

Listen: no recordings of Wiggins himself survive, so the video above carries "The Battle of Manassas" in a later pianist's performance. The upload's title does not name the pianist. Listen for the passages that imitate cannon fire and train whistles.

### 2. Ray Charles (1930-2004) - Father of Soul, Pianist and Singer

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qIp9TwSEgFg

Born Ray Charles Robinson in Albany, Georgia, on 23 September 1930, Ray Charles grew up in Greenville, Florida. He began to lose his sight at about five, and by seven he was blind. The probable cause was glaucoma. His mother enrolled him at the Florida School for the Deaf and the Blind in St. Augustine. He stayed nine years, learned to read music, played Chopin and Beethoven, and came to admire Art Tatum.

He built his career on his voice as much as his piano. On "I Got a Woman" (Atlantic, 1954) he blended gospel, blues and jazz, and the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame credits him with combining gospel and blues into soul. He performed under the billing "the Genius", and Britannica notes that writers have called him the Father of Soul. His version of Hoagy Carmichael's "Georgia on My Mind" reached No. 1 on the pop chart in 1960, and Georgia made it the state song in 1979. In 1962 he turned to country music with Modern Sounds in Country and Western Music, which included "I Can't Stop Loving You".

The Rock & Roll Hall of Fame inducted him in 1986. He died at his home in Beverly Hills on 10 June 2004.

Listen: the video above, a live "Georgia on My Mind" from the official RayCharlesVEVO channel. The upload gives no date or venue.

### 3. Art Tatum (1909-1956) - The Blind Jazz Piano Virtuoso

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D9Cs_zb4q14

Art Tatum, born in Toledo, Ohio, on 13 October 1909, had little sight from childhood because of cataracts. Accounts differ on how much he could see, and some biographers say he kept partial vision in one eye.

He started on violin and moved to piano by 13. He learned a lot by ear and also took formal classical lessons from Overton G. Rainey, a teacher with impaired sight. After a short spell at the Ohio state school for blind students in Columbus, he studied at the Toledo School of Music.

Tatum recorded for Decca from 1934 to 1941. Between December 1953 and 1956 the producer Norman Granz recorded him at length on solo piano, and Pablo later issued those sessions as The Complete Pablo Solo Masterpieces. He died in Los Angeles on 5 November 1956, aged 47.

Listen: the video above, Tatum's "Yesterdays", a Jerome Kern song. The jazz scholar Lewis Porter notes that Tatum kept a set arrangement of it in Kern's original key of D minor. The upload gives no recording date.

### 4. Blind Willie Johnson (1897-1945) - The Soul of Gospel Blues: Singer and Slide Guitarist

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BNj2BXW852g

Blind Willie Johnson sang gospel and played slide guitar. His death certificate gives his birth as 22 January 1897 near Brenham, Texas, and the Handbook of Texas follows it. Other sources give 25 January 1897 at Pendleton, and older reference books gave 1902. Accounts of how he lost his sight conflict, and the Handbook treats the best-known story as legend.

His father made him a cigar-box guitar, and he taught himself to play. He became a master of bottleneck (slide) guitar and sang in what the Handbook calls a "rasping false bass" at Baptist meetings and on the street. Between 1927 and 1930 he made 30 recordings for Columbia in four sessions, including "Nobody's Fault but Mine" and "Dark Was the Night, Cold Was the Ground". In 1977 NASA put "Dark Was the Night" on the Golden Record that travels with the Voyager space probes. He died in Beaumont, Texas, on 18 September 1945.

Listen: Johnson recorded "Dark Was the Night, Cold Was the Ground" in Dallas on 3 December 1927. The piece has no lyrics; you hear his moaning voice follow the slide guitar line.

### 5. Blind John Davis (1913-1985) - Chicago Blues Pianist

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mq9XL0heEFA

John Henry Davis came from Hattiesburg, Mississippi, born there on 7 December 1913, and moved to Chicago with his parents as a small child. He lost his sight at nine, according to the Library of Congress, and took up piano as a teenager.

From 1937 to 1942 he played on recording sessions for the producer Lester Melrose, whose artists recorded for Bluebird, Columbia and Decca. Davis accompanied Big Bill Broonzy, Tampa Red, Memphis Minnie, Red Nelson and John Lee "Sonny Boy" Williamson, often called Sonny Boy Williamson I. He kept touring with blues musicians until his death in Chicago in 1985.

Listen: the video above, which the uploader titles "Chicago (1983)", two years before his death. I could not confirm the venue.

### 6. Stevie Wonder (b. 1950) - Multi-Genre Musical Icon: Singer, Songwriter and Keyboard Player

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=97hwNY3ni10

Stevie Wonder came into the world six weeks premature on 13 May 1950 in Saginaw, Michigan. He lost his sight as a newborn to retinopathy of prematurity; Britannica and Biography.com point to excess oxygen in his incubator as a cause. Motown's Tamla label signed him in 1961, at 11, and "Fingertips (Part 2)" topped the US pop and R&B charts in 1963.

He turned 21 in 1971, when his first contract ran out. He set up his own publishing company, Black Bull Music, and Motown offered a new contract that gave him artistic control. Under that contract he released Music of My Mind and Talking Book (1972), Innervisions (1973), Fulfillingness' First Finale (1974) and Songs in the Key of Life (1976). He sings, writes and plays piano and synthesizers on them. "Superstition", from Talking Book, reached No. 1 and won two Grammys. Songs in the Key of Life includes "Sir Duke", his tribute to Duke Ellington, and the Grammy-winning "I Wish".

Listen: the video above, "Superstition" from Wonder's 1974 concert for the West German TV show Musikladen. German TV listings date the recording to 23 January 1974, and the song closed his set.

### 7. Blind Willie McTell (1898-1959) - Country Blues Storyteller, Guitarist and Singer

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fnWxZtI3ONY

Blind Willie McTell came from Thomson, Georgia. The New Georgia Encyclopedia gives his birth as 5 May 1898; other references give 1901. He was blind from birth or from a young age. He learned guitar from his mother, relatives and neighbours in Statesboro, and attended schools for the blind in Georgia, New York and Michigan.

He moved from six-string to twelve-string guitar, which Atlanta street players liked for its volume. His playing mixed Piedmont blues with ragtime, and the New Georgia Encyclopedia describes his voice as soft and expressive. He recorded for Victor and Columbia from 1927. He died of a cerebral haemorrhage at Milledgeville State Hospital in Georgia on 19 August 1959.

Listen: McTell recorded "Statesboro Blues" for Victor in Atlanta on 17 October 1928, and the Blues Foundation has inducted that recording into its Blues Hall of Fame. The upload above names the song but gives no date.

More blind Black musicians and singers

Al Hibbler (1915-2001). Blind from birth or childhood (reports differ), Hibbler sang with Duke Ellington's orchestra from 1943 to 1951 and made 82 recordings with the band. His 1955 solo version of "Unchained Melody" became a Billboard pop hit.

Reverend Gary Davis (1895 or 1896-1972). Blind from infancy, Davis sang, preached and played guitar on the streets of Durham, North Carolina, and recorded fifteen songs in New York in 1935 as "Blind Gary". He settled in New York in 1944, and his finger-picking guitar style influenced folk-revival players such as Dave Van Ronk.

Blind Lemon Jefferson (mid-1890s-1929). Born near Wortham, Texas, Jefferson sang and played guitar and performed in Dallas's Deep Ellum. He began recording in Chicago in 1926 and made 110 sides, alternate takes included, before his death there in December 1929.

How adversity shaped their music

The sources point to schools, churches and record labels. Schools for blind students taught Charles and McTell, and Tatum took classical lessons from a teacher with impaired sight. Johnson sang at Baptist meetings and in churches, and Charles carried gospel into "I Got a Woman". Columbia, Victor and Bluebird put Johnson, McTell and Davis on records that travelled far beyond their home towns. Some of the money went elsewhere: Wiggins's concerts paid for John Bethune's lifestyle and Eliza Bethune's house.

FAQ

### Who is the most famous blind piano player?

Ray Charles and Stevie Wonder are the best-known names, and both sang as well as played. For jazz piano, the usual answer is Art Tatum, whom Ray Charles admired as a student.

### Who was the blind Black pianist who played jazz?

Art Tatum (1909-1956), from Toledo, Ohio. Cataracts left him with little sight from childhood, and he recorded for Decca in the 1930s and for Norman Granz in the 1950s.

### Who are famous blind Black singers?

Ray Charles, Stevie Wonder, Blind Willie Johnson and Blind Willie McTell, all profiled above. Al Hibbler, Reverend Gary Davis and Blind Lemon Jefferson also sang.

### Was Ray Charles a pianist?

Yes. He studied piano at the Florida School for the Deaf and the Blind and played piano as he sang on his records and on stage.
