The 4 types of online piano lessons

"Online piano lessons" covers four very different things, and choosing the right type matters more than choosing the right brand:

  1. Apps that listen (Simply Piano, Skoove, Flowkey, Piano Marvel, Playground Sessions). They hear you through your device's microphone or a MIDI cable and react instantly. Best for daily practice and motivation.
  2. Video courses (Pianoforall, Pianote, Piano by Pictures). Step-by-step lessons you watch and copy at your own pace. Best for understanding how music works.
  3. Live online teachers (for example, Online Piano Teachers). A real teacher on a video call. Best for technique, posture and accountability.
  4. Free resources (Hoffman Academy, YouTube channels). Great extras, but there's no plan and no feedback.

Most of my adult students do best with a mix: a course or app for daily practice, plus an occasional live lesson to check technique. I call it the hybrid plan, and it's the best value in online piano lessons.

Yes. You can learn piano online, especially as an adult beginner, as long as you practise regularly and get some feedback on your technique. What is the 80/20 rule for piano learning?

The 80/20 rule for piano says that a small set of skills, roughly 20%, gives you about 80% of the music you want to play. It isn't a scientific law, but it's a useful way to plan practice. As a teacher, these are the skills I'd put in that 20%:

  • Four chords: C, G, Am and F (I–V–vi–IV) sit behind a huge number of pop songs.
  • A few left-hand patterns that turn those chords into music.
  • The notes around middle C in both clefs.
  • A steady beat, practised slowly with a metronome.
  • 15 to 20 minutes a day, rather than one long session a week.

Chord-first courses like Pianoforall are built around exactly this idea, which is why, in my teaching experience, adults often reach their first real song sooner with them than with a reading-first method.

What is the quickest way to learn piano?

The quickest way to learn piano is short, focused daily practice with a structured course, starting with chords and simple songs rather than theory alone. Learn one hand at a time, slow down until you can play it right, and add a teacher's feedback when you can.
