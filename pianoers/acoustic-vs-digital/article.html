Quick answer

Is a digital or acoustic piano better?

For most beginners, a digital piano is the better choice. A good one with 88 weighted keys costs about $400 to $700, never needs tuning, weighs about 25 to 40 lb and lets you practise silently with headphones. An acoustic piano is better for sound, touch and long-term classical study, but a good new upright costs about $3,500 to $10,000, needs tuning about twice a year and weighs 300 lb or more. If you want real acoustic touch but quiet practice, look at a hybrid or silent piano.

"Should I buy an acoustic or a digital piano?" is the question I'm asked most, by parents, adult beginners and returning players alike. I've taught since 2001 on everything from century-old uprights to the newest Yamaha, Roland and Kawai digitals. I've paid tuning bills, I've moved pianos up staircases, and I've practised at 2 a.m. with headphones without a single complaint from the neighbours.

So this isn't a sales pitch for either side. Below I compare acoustic and digital pianos on everything that matters: sound, touch, the real cost over ten years, upkeep, space, noise, features and resale. What's the difference between an acoustic and a digital piano?

An acoustic piano makes sound mechanically. Each key works as a lever that throws a felt hammer at steel strings. The strings vibrate, and a large wooden soundboard amplifies that vibration into the sound you hear. Uprights and grands are both acoustic pianos. If you're curious about the mechanism, I explain it step by step in how the piano works.

A digital piano has no strings. Sensors under each key measure how fast you pressed it, and a sound engine plays back the matching sound: either a recording of a real concert piano (sampling) or a computer model of one (physical modelling). The sound comes out through built-in speakers or headphones.

That one difference explains almost everything else in this comparison. Strings and wood sound rich and alive, but they drift out of tune and react to humidity. Sensors and speakers never go out of tune, can be turned down, and fit in a slim case.

Sound: can a digital piano sound like a real piano?

A good digital piano sounds convincingly like a real piano, especially through headphones. But it isn't identical, and the gap is easiest to hear in a room.

  • Resonance. On an acoustic piano, the strings you don't play vibrate in sympathy with the ones you do, especially with the sustain pedal down. Better digitals simulate this "string resonance" and "damper resonance". Cheaper ones don't, which is why they can sound a little flat or dry.
  • Speakers. An acoustic's soundboard fills the room from a large surface. A digital squeezes the sound through small speakers. Console models with bigger speaker systems close much of that gap; portable models struggle with deep bass.
  • Dynamic range. An acoustic responds to every tiny change in how hard you play. Good digitals have many recorded loudness layers or modelled sound, so soft and loud playing sound genuinely different, not just quieter or louder.
  • Headphones level the field. Through good headphones, a well-sampled digital piano can sound wonderful. It's the room sound, not the piano sound, where acoustics pull ahead.

My take: for learning, practising and playing at home, a good digital piano's sound is more than enough. Pianists who mainly play classical repertoire with lots of pedal and soft dynamics will notice the difference most.

Touch and key action: does a digital piano feel like a real piano?

Touch matters more than sound for learning, because it's what trains your fingers. On an acoustic piano, the key you press lifts a real hammer, and the weight you feel comes from that mechanism. Bass keys feel heavier than treble keys, and you can feel the moment the hammer is released (called escapement).

Good digital pianos imitate this with a weighted, graded hammer action: heavier keys in the bass and lighter in the treble, often with a small hammer inside each key. Examples are Yamaha's GHS and GHC actions, Roland's PHA-4, Kawai's Responsive Hammer actions and Casio's Smart Scaled Hammer action. Higher-end models add wooden keys, longer key pivots and three sensors per key for faster repeated notes.

  • Does technique transfer? Yes. Students who learn on a good weighted digital move to an acoustic without trouble. They notice the difference for a week or two, then adapt.
  • What doesn't transfer: unweighted, springy keyboard keys. They don't build finger strength or control, which is why I never recommend a 61-key keyboard for real piano lessons.
  • Where acoustics still win: the finest control of soft playing, fast repetition on a grand, and the feel of the hammer releasing.

The Yamaha P-145 is a good example of a beginner action done right for about $500. I explain how it feels in my Yamaha P-145 review.

Cost: the price to buy and the real cost to own

The sticker price is only part of the story. Over ten years, upkeep and moving change the picture a lot.

Price to buy

  • Digital pianos: about $400 to $700 for a good beginner model, about $1,000 to $3,000 for a better portable or a console with cabinet and three pedals, and more for flagship and hybrid models. My picks at every level are in the best digital pianos, and the beginner-friendly ones are in the best beginner pianos.
  • Acoustic pianos: a good-quality new upright typically costs about $3,500 to $10,000, and a new grand starts around $7,500 and goes far higher. Used pianos can cost much less, but a cheap used piano that needs repairs can cost more than it saves.

Cost over ten years

Tuning is the big recurring cost. The Piano Technicians Guild advises three to four tunings in a new piano's first year, while the strings and wood settle, and at least two a year after that (PTG). At about $100 to $200 a visit in the US, that adds up to thousands of dollars over a decade. A digital piano costs nothing to keep in tune.

The one cost that works in the acoustic's favour is resale. A well-kept acoustic from a good maker can be sold years later for a meaningful part of its price, while most digital pianos are worth little after ten years.

Maintenance: tuning, humidity and care

An acoustic piano is a wooden instrument holding many tons of string tension (often quoted at around 18 to 20 tons in a concert grand), and it reacts to its environment. A digital piano is a piece of electronics. That's the difference in upkeep in a sentence.

Acoustic piano care

  • Tuning on the schedule above. Skipping tunings for years means the piano drops in pitch, and bringing it back up costs extra.
  • Steady humidity. Wood swells and shrinks as humidity changes, which pulls the piano out of tune and can crack the soundboard over time. The Piano Technicians Guild stresses keeping humidity as constant as possible (PTG). Steinway recommends a constant room temperature and avoiding large swings in humidity (Steinway). My full guide is piano humidity and temperature, and the tools are covered in piano humidifiers and piano dehumidifiers.
  • Regulation and voicing every few years, so the keys feel even and the tone stays sweet.
  • Placement: away from radiators, air vents, fireplaces and sunny windows.

For everyday care, read how to clean and maintain your piano and when and why a piano needs tuning.

Digital piano care

Keep it covered from dust, wipe the keys with a barely damp cloth, and install the occasional firmware or app update. That's all. Climate matters far less, though extreme heat or damp isn't good for any electronics.

Space, weight and moving

This is often the deciding factor in apartments and smaller homes.

  • Portable digital pianos weigh about 25 to 40 lb and are about a foot deep. One person can carry one, and it fits on a stand against any wall.
  • Console digital pianos look like furniture, weigh about 75 to 150 lb and are still slim enough for a hallway.
  • Upright pianos weigh about 300 to 800 lb, depending on size, and need piano movers. Moving one up stairs is a job for professionals, not friends.
  • Grand pianos start around 500 to 600 lb for a baby grand and need a large room.

Volume, headphones and neighbours

An acoustic piano is loud, and you can't turn it down. That's wonderful in a house and a real problem in a flat, a dorm or a home with a sleeping baby. Many uprights have a middle "practice" pedal that drops a felt strip between the hammers and strings to muffle the sound, but it changes the touch and is still clearly audible.

A digital piano has a volume control and a headphone jack, often two, so a teacher and student can listen together. For many adult learners, the ability to practise at 10 p.m. without disturbing anyone is the single reason they practise at all.

Features: what digital pianos add

  • Built-in metronome and rhythm patterns.
  • Recording, so you can hear yourself back, one of the best ways to improve.
  • USB or Bluetooth MIDI, which lets learning apps hear exactly what you play. Apps like Flowkey, Skoove, Simply Piano and Piano Marvel all work best this way; see the best piano learning apps.
  • Bluetooth audio, to play along with songs through the piano's speakers.
  • Other sounds: electric pianos, organ, strings and more, plus transpose and split or dual modes.

An acoustic piano has none of these, though you can still use apps that listen through a phone or tablet microphone.

Lifespan and resale value

A well-made acoustic piano can last for generations with good care. Strings, hammers and other parts wear and can be replaced, and fine pianos are rebuilt rather than thrown away. That's why good acoustic pianos keep more of their value.

A digital piano is consumer electronics. Many last ten years or more, but the technology dates, keys can get noisier with heavy use, and replacement parts eventually become hard to find. Plan on using it, not selling it.

Acoustic or digital piano for beginners?

For most beginners, children and adults alike, I recommend a good digital piano. Here's why I say it to new students every week:

  1. You'll practise more. Headphones mean you can play whenever you have twenty minutes, at any hour, without worrying about anyone.
  2. You'll spend less while you find out if you love it. About $500 instead of $5,000.
  3. Technique transfers. A weighted 88-key action trains your fingers properly, and the move to an acoustic later is easy.
  4. It helps you learn. The metronome, recording and app connection are genuinely useful for beginners. My guide to teaching yourself piano uses them at every step.

The beginners I'd steer towards an acoustic are those who already know they're committed, have a stable room and the budget, and want to study classical piano seriously, or families who have been offered a good piano that a technician has checked.

For children: a good digital piano is perfectly suitable for lessons. What matters is 88 weighted, touch-sensitive keys and a proper sustain pedal. If your child progresses quickly through the grades, an acoustic becomes a great upgrade a few years in.

Hybrid and silent pianos: the middle ground

If you want the touch of an acoustic but need to practise quietly, there are two in-between options.

  • Hybrid pianos combine a real wooden acoustic piano action with a digital sound engine. There are no strings, so they never need tuning and can be played with headphones. Examples include Yamaha's AvantGrand, Kawai's Novus and Casio's Grand Hybrid lines. They cost several thousand dollars.
  • Silent pianos are real acoustic uprights or grands with a system that stops the hammers before they hit the strings and plays a digital sound through headphones instead. Yamaha's Silent Piano (SH3) and Kawai's AnyTime (ATX4) are the best-known systems. You get a true acoustic when you want it and silent practice when you need it, but you pay more than for the same piano without the system, and the acoustic side still needs tuning.

Acoustic vs electric piano vs keyboard: clearing up the names

  • Acoustic piano: real strings and hammers (upright or grand).
  • Digital piano: 88 weighted keys designed to feel and sound like an acoustic piano, with speakers and headphones.
  • Electric piano: strictly, vintage instruments like the Rhodes and Wurlitzer, which make sound with metal tines or reeds and pickups. In everyday speech, though, most people who search for an "electric piano" mean a digital piano, and that's what stores sell under the name today.
  • Keyboard: usually 61 lighter, unweighted keys with hundreds of sounds. Fun and cheap, but not a substitute for a piano action if you want to learn properly.

The piano buying checklist

Buying a used acoustic piano

  1. Book a technician's inspection before you pay. It costs a fraction of what a bad piano will.
  2. Check the pitch. A piano far below standard pitch needs a pitch raise as well as tuning.
  3. Look and listen for problems: soundboard cracks, loose tuning pins, sticking keys, buzzes, worn hammers.
  4. Try every key soft and loud, and all the pedals. When you lift a key, the sound should stop cleanly.
  5. Add moving and a first tuning to the asking price. A "free" piano is rarely free.

For small fixes you can do yourself, see my DIY piano repair guide.

Buying a digital piano

  1. 88 fully weighted, hammer-action keys. This is non-negotiable for learning.
  2. Adjustable touch sensitivity, so you can match your playing strength.
  3. At least 64-note polyphony; 128 or more is better for pedalled music.
  4. A real piano-style sustain pedal. Replace the small square footswitch many models ship with.
  5. Headphone and USB or Bluetooth MIDI connections for quiet practice and apps.
  6. A sturdy stand and bench at the right height.

Acoustic vs digital piano: FAQ

Is a digital piano as good as an acoustic piano?

For learning and everyday practice, a good digital piano with 88 weighted keys is as good as most people need, and technique learned on it transfers to an acoustic. An acoustic piano still has richer sound, natural resonance and the finest touch, which matter most for advanced classical playing.

Should a beginner buy an acoustic or digital piano?

Most beginners should start with a digital piano. It costs about $400 to $700, needs no tuning, allows silent practice with headphones and connects to learning apps. An acoustic makes sense for committed students with the budget, space and a stable room.

Can you learn piano on a digital piano?

Yes. Many teachers, including me, are happy for students to learn on a digital piano, as long as it has 88 fully weighted, touch-sensitive keys and a proper sustain pedal. Avoid unweighted keyboards for real lessons.

How often does an acoustic piano need tuning?

The Piano Technicians Guild recommends three to four tunings in a new piano's first year and at least two a year after that. Digital pianos never need tuning.

Do acoustic pianos hold their value better than digital pianos?

Generally yes. A well-maintained acoustic from a good maker can resell for a meaningful part of its price, while most digital pianos lose most of their value within a few years.

Is a digital piano cheaper than an acoustic piano in the long run?

Usually, yes. Over ten years, a digital piano costs little beyond its purchase price, while an acoustic adds tuning, moving and humidity control, often thousands of dollars. Resale value narrows the gap for good acoustics.

What is the difference between a digital piano and a keyboard?

A digital piano has 88 weighted, hammer-action keys designed to feel like a real piano. A keyboard usually has 61 lighter, unweighted keys and many sounds. For learning piano technique, a digital piano is much better.

What is a hybrid piano?

A hybrid piano combines a real wooden acoustic piano action with a digital sound engine and no strings. It feels very close to an acoustic, never needs tuning and can be played with headphones, but costs several thousand dollars.

Is an electric piano the same as a digital piano?

In everyday use, yes: most people and stores use "electric piano" to mean a digital piano. Strictly, electric pianos are vintage instruments like the Rhodes and Wurlitzer, which make sound mechanically with tines or reeds and pickups.

Can an acoustic piano be played quietly?

Partly. Many uprights have a practice pedal that muffles the sound, though it's still audible. For truly silent practice, a silent piano system such as Yamaha's SH3 or Kawai's AnyTime adds headphone playing to a real acoustic.

My verdict

Buy a digital piano if you're a beginner or returning adult, live in an apartment, practise at odd hours, want to test the waters, or want apps and recording. Buy an acoustic piano if you have the space, budget and a stable room, you're committed for the long term, and the sound and touch of real strings matter to you. Consider a hybrid or silent piano if you want both, and the budget allows.
