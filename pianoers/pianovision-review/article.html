PianoVision (you'll also see it written as Piano Vision) is the app that made me take VR piano seriously. You put on a Meta Quest, sit at your own piano, and coloured notes fall straight onto the real keys in front of you. No screen to glance at, no tablet balanced on the music rest. As a piano teacher I was sceptical, so I set it up the way my students would: with a digital piano, then the virtual keyboard, then an acoustic. This review covers what PianoVision does well, where it falls short, what it really costs in 2026, and how to set it up on a Meta Quest 3 or Quest 3S, desktop app included.

What is PianoVision?

PianoVision is a mixed reality piano learning app for Meta Quest headsets. It uses the headset's passthrough cameras, so you still see your room, your piano and your hands. On top of that view it draws a virtual layer: notes that fall onto the exact keys you need to play, a song menu, optional sheet music and your scores.

It was created by developer Zac Reid, who is now the company's founder and CEO. PianoVision started in early access on Quest back in 2022. The big version 2.0 update arrived at the end of 2024 with a redesigned interface, the PianoVision Plus song library, curated mixed reality environments and an AI piano teacher called Ludwig. The companion desktop app has since moved on to version 3.

If you have seen falling-note piano videos on YouTube, or used Synthesia, you already know the idea. The difference is where the notes are. In Synthesia they fall on a screen. In PianoVision they fall on your piano. That one change matters more than it sounds, and I'll explain why below.

How PianoVision works

The first time you start PianoVision at your keyboard, you calibrate it: you line up the virtual keyboard with your real one so the app knows exactly where every key is. After that, a session looks like this:

  1. Pick a song or exercise. Basic includes over 1,000 public domain songs and exercises, plus hundreds of chords and scales.
  2. Watch the notes fall. Each bar drops onto the key you need. Its length shows how long to hold the note, and the two hands have different colours.
  3. Use Wait mode first. The music stops on each note until you play it correctly, so you learn the shape of a piece without pressure.
  4. Play along and check your stats. PianoVision records timing, accuracy and handedness, so you can see which bars need work.

Why does this work so well for beginners? With a normal app, your eyes jump between a screen and your hands dozens of times a minute. Every jump costs you your place. In PianoVision the instruction sits exactly where your fingers are. My adult beginners tend to lose their place most at exactly that moment, when they look down from the music to the keys and back up again. PianoVision simply removes the problem.

Three ways to play: MIDI keyboard, virtual keyboard or acoustic piano

PianoVision says you can play "using your Piano, MIDI Keyboard, or if you don't have either, then our Virtual Keyboard using hand-tracking." In practice the three options feel very different.

1. With a MIDI keyboard or digital piano (the best way)

This is how PianoVision is meant to be used. Connected by MIDI, the app knows exactly which key you pressed and when. Wait mode works properly, your scores are accurate, and you get real key weight under your fingers. Any digital piano with a USB "to host" port, like the Yamaha P-145, is a good candidate. If you still need a keyboard, start with my list of the best beginner pianos or the best digital pianos overall.

2. With the virtual keyboard (no piano needed)

Without any keyboard, PianoVision draws a virtual piano on a table or in the air and tracks your fingers with the headset cameras. It's a fun way to try the app, and it shows you note patterns. But there is no key weight and no resistance, and hand tracking is less precise than a cable. I treat it as a demo, not as practice.

3. With an acoustic piano

You can calibrate PianoVision to an acoustic piano and see the notes fall onto its keys. However, PianoVision's official setup guides all rely on MIDI for note input, and I found no microphone note detection in its material. So with a plain acoustic piano the app can show you the notes, but it can't hear what you play. Wait mode and scoring need a MIDI signal. If your acoustic has no MIDI, it is a visual guide only.

How to connect a MIDI keyboard to PianoVision

There are two official ways to connect a keyboard, plus a Bluetooth workaround.

Option 1: USB cable straight into the headset

This is the simplest setup and the one I recommend. PianoVision's guide says the cable is "most commonly" a USB-B (printer cable) to USB-C cable. The USB-B end goes into the keyboard's "USB to host" port, and the USB-C end goes into the Quest. If your keyboard has a USB-C port, use a USB-C to USB-C cable instead. No computer and no Wi-Fi involved.

Option 2: The free PianoVision desktop app

Your keyboard plugs into a Windows PC or Mac over USB, and the computer passes the notes to the headset over your home network. More on this in the next section.

Bluetooth keyboards

The Quest doesn't connect to Bluetooth MIDI keyboards directly. PianoVision's keyboard compatibility list mentions using a Bluetooth MIDI dongle such as the CME WIDI Bud Pro instead. Older keyboards with 5-pin DIN MIDI ports can connect through a MIDI-to-USB adapter cable.

Before you buy a cable or keyboard, check PianoVision's keyboard compatibility list. It shows, model by model, whether a keyboard works by USB, DIN, the desktop app or Bluetooth, and whether it has its own speakers. If it doesn't, you'll hear PianoVision's built-in sounds instead, or you can run a VST on a computer.

The PianoVision desktop app

The PianoVision desktop app is a free companion app for Windows and Mac, with separate downloads for Windows (x64), Macs with Apple M-series chips, and Intel Macs. When I checked the downloads in October 2026, they were on version 3.0.28. You'll find them on PianoVision's resources page.

You only need it for two things, as PianoVision puts it: to "connect your VR headset to your computer" or to "upload your own MIDI files to the app."

How to connect the desktop app to your Quest:

  1. Install the desktop app and connect your keyboard to the computer by USB.
  2. Make sure the computer and the Quest are on the same network.
  3. Start PianoVision in the headset. The desktop app will usually find the Quest automatically.
  4. If it asks for a Connect Code, enter the last three digits of the Quest's IP address.

How to upload your own songs: connect the desktop app to the headset as above, then upload a MIDI file. It appears in your Imports library in the headset. PianoVision suggests MuseScore.com as a good source of MIDI files. Basic includes 100 MIDI imports, and Plus adds auto-generated sheet music and finger numbers.

My advice: use the USB cable for everyday practice and keep the desktop app for importing songs. A cable has fewer things that can go wrong than a Wi-Fi link.

PianoVision features, explained

Practice modes

Beyond the classic falling notes, PianoVision has a Wait mode that pauses until you play the right note, ghost hands that show finger placement, a sheet music mode, and performance feedback for timing and accuracy. You can practise hands separately and slow a song down too.

The Learning Engine

PianoVision's Learning Engine "responds to your real-time performance, breaks songs into snippets, and restructures the piece" to suit your practice. In other words, it does what I ask students to do: don't play a piece from the top every time, work on the bars you get wrong. You can also select a few measures and loop them, with a history of your scores for each attempt.

The Memory Engine

This is the feature I like most as a teacher. The Memory Engine is "designed to incrementally reduce your dependency on the VR headset." The help fades out gradually, so the song moves from the screen into your memory. Good teachers do the same thing: show it, then step back. It's the best answer I've seen to the biggest weakness of falling-note apps, which is that you can end up following lights rather than learning music.

Ludwig, the AI piano teacher

Version 2.0 added Ludwig, an AI teacher. It gives real-time feedback, answers questions about technique, recommends songs and adapts to your level. Basic includes a basic AI assistant, and Plus has the full "contextual" one. It's handy for quick questions in the middle of practice. But it can't see that your wrist is collapsing or your shoulders are up by your ears. That's still a job for a human teacher.

Songs, multiplayer and environments

Basic has 1,000+ public domain songs and exercises, a jazz song generator and a holiday music pack. Plus adds 10,000+ popular songs from more than 1,500 artists, including The Beatles, Disney, John Legend and Ed Sheeran, organised into 400+ playlists. There's also low-latency multiplayer to play together with people in other places, and Plus adds custom mixed reality environments that change the look of your room.

Can PianoVision really teach you piano?

This is the big question, so here is my honest answer as a teacher: PianoVision is an excellent practice tool and a weak complete method.

What it does well:

  • It removes the eye-jumping problem. Notes sit on your keys, so you keep your place.
  • It keeps you motivated. Popular songs, scores and short loops make practice feel like a game. Motivation is what keeps most learners going in the first months.
  • It practises the right way. Wait mode, slow tempos, hands separately and looping weak bars are exactly what I'd ask a student to do.

Where it falls short:

  • Reading music. Falling notes are easier than notation, so it's easy to never learn to read. Use the sheet music mode, and also practise from real scores.
  • Technique. No app can see tension, posture, wrist height or the way you use the weight of your arm. Bad habits are easy to build alone.
  • Theory and musicianship. Chords and scales are included, but there's no full curriculum that explains why the music works.
  • Comfort. A headset gets warm and heavy. I find shorter, focused sessions work better than long ones.

My recommendation: use PianoVision to learn songs and keep practice fun, and pair it with something that builds reading, such as an app like Piano Marvel, a good method book, or a few lessons with a teacher. PianoVision vs Simply Piano, Flowkey and Piano Marvel

If you're wondering how to learn piano online, PianoVision is just one option. Here is how it compares with the apps I recommend most often:

  • Simply Piano is better for total beginners who want a guided path on a phone or tablet.
  • Flowkey is better if you want to learn songs while also seeing sheet music and videos of real hands.
  • Piano Marvel is better if reading music and structured progress matter most to you.
  • PianoVision is the best choice if you already own a Quest and want the most immersive way to learn songs.

Who should buy PianoVision?

Buy PianoVision if you already own a Meta Quest 3 or 3S, you have a digital piano or MIDI keyboard, and you want to learn songs quickly in a way that feels like a game. It's also a great extra for teens and adults who have stalled with screen-based apps.

Skip it if you don't want a headset, you get motion sick, your main goal is fluent sight-reading, or you only have an acoustic piano without MIDI. In those cases a regular app or a teacher will serve you better.

PianoVision FAQ

How much does PianoVision cost?

PianoVision Basic is a one-time purchase of $9.99 on the Meta Horizon Store. The optional PianoVision Plus subscription costs $9.99 a month or $99.99 a year and adds 10,000+ popular songs. Prices are in USD, checked in October 2026.

Is PianoVision free?

No. There is no free version of the Quest app: you need PianoVision Basic for $9.99. The PianoVision desktop app for Windows and Mac is free.

Does PianoVision work on Meta Quest 3 and Quest 3S?

Yes. PianoVision runs on the Meta Quest 3, Quest 3S, Quest Pro and Quest 2. The Quest 3 gives the clearest passthrough view of your keys, and the Quest 3S is the best value.

Do I need a piano to use PianoVision?

No, you can use the virtual keyboard with hand tracking. But for real practice, a MIDI keyboard or digital piano is much better, because the app can hear exactly what you play and you get real key weight.

Can I use PianoVision with an acoustic piano?

You can calibrate it to an acoustic piano and see the notes fall onto its keys. PianoVision's official setup guides rely on MIDI for note input, so without MIDI the app can't hear what you play, and Wait mode and scoring won't work.

How do I connect my keyboard to PianoVision?

The easiest way is a USB cable from the keyboard straight to the headset, usually a USB-B to USB-C cable. You can also connect the keyboard to a PC or Mac and link it to the Quest with the free PianoVision desktop app over your home network.

What is the PianoVision desktop app for?

The free desktop app for Windows and Mac does two things: it connects a keyboard to the headset through your computer, and it uploads your own MIDI files to PianoVision. If it can't find your Quest, enter the Connect Code, which is the last three digits of the headset's IP address.

Can I use a Bluetooth keyboard with PianoVision?

Not directly, because the Quest doesn't support Bluetooth MIDI connections. PianoVision's compatibility list mentions using a Bluetooth MIDI dongle such as the CME WIDI Bud Pro instead.

Is PianoVision available on Apple Vision Pro?

I couldn't find a PianoVision app for Apple Vision Pro. PianoVision lists only Meta Quest headsets: Quest 3, Quest 3S, Quest Pro and Quest 2.

Can I upload my own songs to PianoVision?

Yes. Upload MIDI files through the desktop app and they appear in your Imports library in the headset. PianoVision Basic includes 100 MIDI imports.

Can you really learn piano in VR?

You can learn to play songs in VR, and PianoVision makes that faster and more fun. To become a well-rounded pianist you still need to learn to read music and build good technique, so pair it with sheet music practice, a structured app or a teacher.
